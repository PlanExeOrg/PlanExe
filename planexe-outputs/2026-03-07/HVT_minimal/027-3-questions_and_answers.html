1. What is the critical regulatory distinction between obtaining a Part 107 pilot license and ensuring the drone hardware itself is legally certified for operations near people?

There is a significant confusion in the plan between operator certification and airworthiness. A FAA Part 107 license certifies the *pilot* to fly, but it does not automatically authorize the specific drone aircraft to operate over people without separate airworthiness certification (Part 107 Subpart E) or a special waiver. The document notes that relying on custom collision sensors is insufficient for legal clearance; the hardware itself must be verified as compliant for over-person operations to avoid immediate cease-and-desist orders and fines.

2. Why is securing binding insurance quotes considered a critical pre-requisite before signing warehouse leases or purchasing hardware in this venture?

Insurance feasibility is a primary gatekeeper for financial viability. The document highlights that high-speed drone operations near people are classified as an 'extreme hazard,' leading to potential coverage denial or premium spikes exceeding $45,000 annually. Since insurers require proof of regulatory compliance (like FAA waivers) before underwriting, but CAPEX spending requires funding/insurance approval, there is a circular dependency risk that necessitates securing binding Letters of Intent from insurers before committing capital.

3. How does the proposed cybersecurity strategy address the specific risk of real-time drone control hijacking during gameplay?

The plan mandates AES-256 encryption for stored customer data but expert review indicates this is insufficient for physical safety. To prevent mid-flight hijacking via RF spoofing, the system requires Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) for mutual authentication between controllers and drones on the control channel itself. The document acknowledges that treating security as a post-integration audit creates vulnerabilities where attackers could turn safety devices into weapons.

5. What specific operational risks make the Dallas-Fort Worth location less favorable than Atlanta or Pittsburgh for this drone venture?

The DFW location poses high airspace risk due to its proximity to DFW International Airport. This triggers Class B airspace restrictions and potential no-fly zones during national security events, creating a high likelihood of launch delays (2-4 months) and fines up to $10,000 per violation. In contrast, Atlanta and Pittsburgh offer industrial zones with lower regulatory friction regarding controlled airspace access.
